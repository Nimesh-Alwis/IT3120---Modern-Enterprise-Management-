# Lecture 05 — Week 05: Introduction to Financial Accounting

## Purpose and accounting equation

Financial accounting records, summarizes, and communicates an organization's financial position and performance to decision-makers.

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

- **Assets:** resources controlled by the business.
- **Liabilities:** present obligations to outsiders.
- **Equity:** owners' residual interest after liabilities.
- Transactions must keep the equation balanced.

Financial accounting follows four broad activities: **recording, classifying, summarizing, and reporting** transactions. It provides structured information for:

- External users: investors/shareholders, lenders, suppliers, regulators, tax authorities, and potential investors.
- Internal users: managers making investment, pricing, project, cost, financing, and viability decisions.

### Essential account classifications

| Category | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Amount earned from selling goods/services before expenses | Subscription, consulting, service revenue |
| Expense | Cost consumed in current operations | Salary, rent, cloud fee, utilities |
| Expenditure | Any money spent; may create an expense or an asset | Equipment purchase is expenditure but initially an asset |
| Current asset | Expected to become cash/be used within one year | Cash, receivables, inventory |
| Non-current/fixed asset | Long-term operating resource | Server, building, vehicle, equipment |
| Intangible asset | Identifiable non-physical value | Patent, trademark; goodwill under relevant rules |
| Current liability | Due within one year | Payables, short-term loan, accrued expense |
| Long-term liability | Due after one year | Long-term bank loan, mortgage, bonds |

`Profit (or loss) = Revenue − Expenses`; a negative amount is a loss. `Equity = Assets − Liabilities` and may include owner/shareholder contributions plus retained earnings.

## Financial statements

### Statement of financial position (balance sheet)

A snapshot at a date. It shows current/non-current assets, current/non-current liabilities, and equity. **Working capital = current assets − current liabilities.**

### Income statement (profit and loss)

Shows performance over a period.

- Revenue − cost of sales = gross profit.
- Gross profit − operating expenses = operating profit.
- Profit is based on accrual accounting and is not the same as cash received.

Profit levels:

- **Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales**: profitability of producing/delivering the offering.
- **Operating profit = Gross profit − Operating expenses**: result from core operations.
- **Net profit:** final result after remaining finance, tax, and other relevant items.
- Compare multiple periods and margins; an absolute profit rise may result only from larger sales while margins weaken.

### Cash-flow statement

Explains cash changes through:

- **Operating activities:** core trading operations.
- **Investing activities:** purchase/sale of long-term assets or investments.
- **Financing activities:** capital, borrowing, repayment, and distributions.

### Statement relationships

Profit generally increases equity; dividends reduce equity. The closing cash balance connects to the balance sheet. Non-cash items such as depreciation affect profit but are adjusted when deriving operating cash flow.

## Accrual concepts

- **Accrual basis:** recognize revenue when earned and expense when incurred.
- **Matching:** associate expenses with the revenue/period they help generate.
- **Depreciation:** allocate an asset's depreciable amount over useful life; it is not a cash payment in the period charged.
- Distinguish **capital expenditure** (long-term benefit) from **revenue expenditure** (current-period operation).

## Financial ratios

- Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities.
- Quick ratio = (current assets − inventory) / current liabilities.
- Gross profit margin = gross profit / revenue × 100.
- Net profit margin = net profit / revenue × 100.
- Return on assets = net profit / total assets × 100.
- Debt-to-equity = total debt / equity.
- Receivable or inventory ratios help assess operational efficiency.

### Ratio families and interpretation

| Family | Question answered | Examples / interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Profitability | Can the firm turn activity/resources into profit? | Gross margin, net margin, ROA/ROE; higher is generally stronger but context matters |
| Liquidity | Can short-term obligations be paid? | Current and quick ratios; very low may mean distress, very high may mean idle resources |
| Efficiency/activity | Are working capital and assets used well? | Receivable collection, inventory turnover, payables period, asset turnover |
| Gearing/leverage | How heavily is the business debt financed? | Debt-to-equity; higher debt can raise return and expansion capacity but also risk |

Ratios need comparison with past periods, targets, and industry peers. A single ratio rarely gives a complete conclusion.

## IT decision examples

- Buying servers may create capital expenditure and depreciation; cloud subscriptions are commonly operating expenses.
- A profitable software firm can still face a cash shortage when customers pay late.
- Project evaluation must consider timing, cash flows, uncertainty, and opportunity cost—not accounting profit alone.

## Worked lecture-style calculations

Given revenue LKR 100,000, cost of sales LKR 60,000, operating expenses LKR 20,000, assets LKR 150,000, and liabilities LKR 90,000:

- Gross profit = 100,000 − 60,000 = **LKR 40,000**.
- Operating profit = 40,000 − 20,000 = **LKR 20,000**; core activity is profitable before other charges.
- Equity = 150,000 − 90,000 = **LKR 60,000**.

Given customer receipts 80,000; salary/operating payments 50,000; equipment purchase 20,000; bank loan received 30,000; loan repayment 10,000:

- Operating cash flow = 80,000 − 50,000 = **+30,000**.
- Investing cash flow = **−20,000**.
- Financing cash flow = 30,000 − 10,000 = **+20,000**.
- Net increase in cash = 30,000 − 20,000 + 20,000 = **+30,000**.

## Dialog Axiata lecture case

Illustrative values: revenue LKR 180bn, cost of sales 90bn, gross profit 90bn, operating expenses 50bn, operating profit 40bn, net profit 20bn; assets 286bn, liabilities 220bn, equity 66bn; operating/investing/financing cash flows +49bn/−20bn/−10bn, giving +19bn net cash flow. The slides interpret gross margin 50%, net margin about 11%, current ratio 2:1, and debt-to-equity about 2.3:1 as strong operating performance and cash generation, good liquidity, efficient collections, and relatively high but manageable infrastructure-related gearing.

Do not conclude from one figure alone: combine profitability, position, liquidity, efficiency, cash flow, trend, peer comparison, and industry structure.

## Exam reminders

- Memorize the accounting equation and the purpose of each statement.
- Do not treat profit and cash flow as identical.
- State the formula, calculate carefully, and interpret what a ratio means.
- Classify each item before calculating; keep statement-of-position values separate from performance-period values.
