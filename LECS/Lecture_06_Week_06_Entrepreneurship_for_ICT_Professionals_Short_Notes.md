# Lecture 06 — Week 06: Entrepreneurship for ICT Professionals

## Entrepreneurship and mindset

Entrepreneurship is the process of identifying an opportunity, mobilizing resources, accepting calculated risk, and creating value through a new or improved venture. Entrepreneurs show initiative, creativity, resilience, learning orientation, ethical judgment, and tolerance for uncertainty.

An **idea** becomes an **opportunity** when it solves a meaningful problem for a reachable market through a feasible and sustainable model.

### Career, entrepreneurship, and intrapreneurship

| Traditional employment | Entrepreneurship |
|---|---|
| Works within an established organization and assigned responsibilities | Creates and leads a new venture around an opportunity |
| More predictable salary, benefits, and risk | Greater uncertainty, control, and upside/downside |
| Career growth within an existing structure | Growth depends on validating and scaling a business |

An **intrapreneur** behaves entrepreneurially inside an existing organization—developing innovations with company resources and lower personal financial risk, while operating under organizational constraints. An entrepreneur owns/leads a separate venture and bears its risk.

### Entrepreneurial mindset

- Opportunity recognition: observe unmet needs, friction, trends, and underserved users.
- Creativity and innovation: create a new or meaningfully improved solution/business model.
- Calculated risk-taking: gather evidence and manage risk rather than gamble blindly.
- Resilience and perseverance: learn from rejection, failure, and changing conditions.
- Customer-centric thinking: validate value with real users rather than technical enthusiasm alone.
- Continuous learning and adaptability: update skills, assumptions, product, and model.

## Entrepreneurial process

1. Observe problems, trends, and unmet needs.
2. Screen the opportunity: desirability, feasibility, viability, and timing.
3. Define customer segments and the value proposition.
4. Test critical assumptions with evidence.
5. Design the business/revenue model.
6. Acquire team, technology, finance, partners, and legal support.
7. Launch, measure, learn, adapt, and scale responsibly.

### Stages and key outputs

| Stage | Main question | Typical output |
|---|---|---|
| Idea generation | What problem/trend can create value? | Problem–solution ideas |
| Opportunity identification | Is there a real customer and valuable gap? | Initial customer/competitor evidence |
| Feasibility analysis | Can it work and survive? | Technical, market, operational, financial assessment |
| Business planning | How will the venture create and capture value? | Mission, customer, solution, revenue, marketing, operations, finance, risk plan |
| Product development/validation | Do users actually value it? | Prototype/MVP, pilot, feedback, learning |
| Business launch | How will we acquire and serve customers? | Market entry, promotion, sales channels, operations |
| Growth/scaling | Can revenue/reach grow efficiently? | New markets/products, automation, partnerships, investment |

Feasibility dimensions must all be checked: **technical** capability, **market** demand/competition, **operational** ability/resources, and **financial** cost/revenue/funding.

## ICT opportunities in Sri Lanka

Potential areas include software services, SaaS, fintech, health-tech, education technology, agriculture technology, tourism platforms, cybersecurity, AI/data solutions, logistics, BPO/KPO, and export-oriented digital products. Local problems can be tested locally and, where applicable, adapted for regional or global markets.

Enablers include technical talent, connectivity, diaspora links, incubators, universities, and global digital distribution. Challenges include finance, market size, policy uncertainty, payment access, skills gaps, talent retention, intellectual property, cybersecurity, and customer trust.

Opportunity drivers in the slides include smartphone/internet access, AI solutions, cloud-based delivery, digital payments, e-commerce growth, remote work, government digitization, and demand for automation. Digital products have few geographical limits, relatively low replication/distribution cost, and global scalability, but localization, regulation, support, infrastructure, and trust still matter.

Local examples:

- **99X:** Sri Lankan product engineering for international markets.
- **PickMe:** localized ride-hailing expanded into food delivery/logistics.
- **Kapruka:** e-commerce serving local buyers and overseas Sri Lankans, then expanding offerings.

Global examples in the lecture—Google, Facebook/Meta, and Uber—illustrate starting from a clear digital value proposition and expanding the platform/model.

## Lean Startup

- **Build–Measure–Learn:** build the smallest useful experiment, measure real behavior, and learn whether assumptions hold.
- **MVP:** minimum viable product used to test the most important hypothesis; it is not simply a poor-quality final product.
- **Validated learning:** evidence that reduces business uncertainty.
- **Pivot:** a structured change in direction while retaining learning; **persevere** when evidence supports the model.

Useful metrics should be actionable and cohort-based; vanity metrics can look impressive without proving progress.

Practical cycle: state a falsifiable assumption → define success metric → build the smallest valid test/MVP → expose it to real target users → measure behavior → learn → pivot or persevere. A 50-user pilot of a farmer app is product development and validation, not full growth/scaling.

## Business model essentials

The Business Model Canvas considers customer segments, value propositions, channels, relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, partners, and cost structure. Common ICT revenue models include subscription, transaction fee, licensing, freemium, advertising, marketplace commission, and professional services.

## Finance, growth, and responsibility

Funding may come from bootstrapping, friends/family, grants, crowdfunding, angel investors, venture capital, bank finance, or strategic partners. Each differs in cost, risk, control, and expectations.

ICT founders should address privacy, security, accessibility, fairness, labor impacts, taxation, licensing, and intellectual property from the start. Growth without governance can magnify harm and operational risk.

## Startup challenges and risk management

Major challenges covered in the lecture:

- **Funding:** limited traction makes investors cautious; funds are needed for technology, people, operations, and marketing.
- **Competition:** established/global firms have capital, brand, data, and customers; startups need differentiation, niche focus, or superior experience.
- **Talent retention:** larger/global employers may offer higher pay and security; culture, ownership, learning, flexibility, and mission can help.
- **Market uncertainty:** demand and preferences are not fully known; continuous validation is essential.
- **Technology:** reliability, scalability, integration, rapid obsolescence, and cybersecurity require investment.
- **Regulatory barriers:** privacy, cybercrime, intellectual property, tax, digital transactions, and sector rules consume scarce capacity.

Risk groups are **technical**, **financial**, **operational**, and **market** risks. Manage them through pilot/MVP testing, a risk register (risk, probability, impact, owner, response, trigger), backups/contingencies, diversified finance/revenue, secure/scalable design, skilled advisers/partners, and continuous market feedback.

## Lessons from success stories

Successful ICT ventures tend to identify a real problem, use technology for a differentiated solution, focus on customer value/service quality, adapt using market evidence, and develop a scalable and financially sustainable model.
