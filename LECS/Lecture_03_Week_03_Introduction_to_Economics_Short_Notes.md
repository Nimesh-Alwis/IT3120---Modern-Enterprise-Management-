# Lecture 03 — Week 03: Introduction to Economics

## Foundations

Economics studies how scarce resources are allocated among competing wants.

- **Scarcity** forces choices.
- **Opportunity cost** is the value of the best alternative forgone.
- **Microeconomics** examines individuals, firms, and markets.
- **Macroeconomics** examines economy-wide output, inflation, unemployment, growth, and policy.

The fundamental economic questions are **what** to produce, **how** to produce it, and **for whom** it should be produced. Factors of production include land/natural resources, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship/technology.

### Markets and the circular flow

- A **market** is any physical or virtual arrangement bringing buyers and sellers together.
- In the basic neoclassical model, households supply factors of production (land, labor, capital) to firms through factor markets and receive wages, rent, interest, and profit.
- Firms use these factors to produce goods/services sold to households through product markets; household expenditure becomes firm revenue.
- The simplified neoclassical model assumes rational choice, supply/demand price signals, many participants, information, and competitive entry/exit. Real markets may depart from these assumptions.

## Demand and supply

- The **law of demand**: other things equal, quantity demanded falls when price rises.
- Demand shifts with income, tastes, expectations, population, and prices of substitutes or complements.
- The **law of supply**: other things equal, quantity supplied rises when price rises.
- Supply shifts with input costs, technology, taxes/subsidies, expectations, and number of sellers.
- **Equilibrium** occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. A shortage puts upward pressure on price; a surplus puts downward pressure on price.

A price change causes movement **along** a curve; a non-price factor shifts the entire curve.

## Elasticity

Price elasticity of demand measures responsiveness: percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in price.

- Elastic demand: absolute elasticity greater than 1.
- Inelastic demand: absolute elasticity less than 1.
- Unit elastic: absolute elasticity equals 1.

Availability of substitutes, necessity, budget share, and time affect elasticity. SaaS firms use elasticity when testing subscriptions and discounts.

Revenue implication:

- With elastic demand, a price rise tends to reduce total revenue and a price reduction can increase it.
- With inelastic demand, a price rise tends to increase total revenue.
- Elasticity is sensitivity, not simply the direction described by the law of demand.

## Production and costs

- **Fixed costs** do not change with short-run output.
- **Variable costs** change with output.
- **Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost.**
- **Average cost = total cost / quantity.**
- **Marginal cost** is the additional cost of one more unit.
- **Sunk costs** have already occurred and should not drive future choices.

### Formula sheet

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total cost | `TC = FC + VC` |
| Average fixed cost | `AFC = FC / Q` |
| Average variable cost | `AVC = VC / Q` |
| Average total cost | `ATC = TC / Q = AFC + AVC` |
| Marginal cost | `MC = ΔTC / ΔQ` |

- **Diminishing marginal product:** after some point, adding more variable input to fixed capacity produces progressively smaller extra output.
- As output grows, AFC continuously falls. AVC commonly eventually rises; together they make ATC U-shaped.
- If `MC < ATC`, ATC falls; if `MC > ATC`, ATC rises. Therefore MC crosses ATC at ATC's minimum.

Digital products often have high initial development cost and low marginal replication cost. Economies of scale reduce average cost as output grows; diseconomies can appear when complexity and coordination increase.

## Market structures

- **Perfect competition:** many sellers and homogeneous products.
- **Monopolistic competition:** many differentiated sellers.
- **Oligopoly:** a few interdependent firms.
- **Monopoly:** a single dominant supplier with strong entry barriers.

Network effects, data advantages, switching costs, standards, and intellectual property influence competition in IT markets.

## Economic benefits and surplus

- **Consumer surplus = willingness to pay − actual price.** Example: willingness to pay $50 and price $30 gives $20 consumer surplus.
- **Producer surplus = price received − cost of production.** Example: price $30 and cost $20 gives $10 producer surplus.
- **Total economic surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus.** It indicates gains created by market exchange, subject to distribution and externality concerns.

## Macroeconomics

- **GDP:** value of final goods and services produced within a country.
- **Inflation:** sustained increase in the general price level.
- **Unemployment:** people willing and able to work but unable to find work.
- **Fiscal policy:** government spending and taxation.
- **Monetary policy:** money supply and interest-rate actions by the central bank.
- Exchange rates, inflation, interest rates, and growth affect IT wages, imports, exports, investment, and project budgets.

## AI and cost decisions

AI can categorize transactions, estimate fixed/variable costs, forecast resource use, find inefficiencies, simulate production levels, and detect cost anomalies. Managers must verify input data, assumptions, unusual results, and context. Automated estimates do not eliminate opportunity cost or managerial accountability.

## Common scenario logic

- Own-price change → movement along the demand/supply curve.
- Income, tastes, related-product price, population, or expectations → demand shift.
- Input cost, technology, number of sellers, tax/subsidy, or expectations → supply shift.
- Demand rises while supply is unchanged → equilibrium price and quantity normally rise.
- Supply rises while demand is unchanged → equilibrium price normally falls and quantity rises.
