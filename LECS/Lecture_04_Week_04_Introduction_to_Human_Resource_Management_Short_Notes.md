# Lecture 04 — Week 04: Introduction to Human Resource Management

## HRM purpose

Human Resource Management attracts, develops, motivates, evaluates, rewards, and retains people so employee capability supports organizational strategy.

HRM is a **strategic and coherent** approach to people as organizational assets, not merely payroll and administration. Its connected elements include employment/resourcing, development, performance management, rewards, employee relations, and well-being.

### Why HRM matters particularly in IT

- Specialized developers, engineers, analysts, cybersecurity experts, and designers create competitive advantage.
- HR aligns skills with innovation, digital transformation, client work, and project deadlines.
- Rapid technological change requires continuous upskilling and career development.
- Global/remote hiring increases both the available talent pool and retention competition.
- Cross-functional Agile teams need collaboration, role clarity, feedback, and conflict management.
- High workload can produce burnout; well-being and work–life balance affect sustainable performance.
- HR supports organizational change through communication, readiness, training, and alignment.

## Workforce planning, recruitment, and selection

1. Forecast workforce demand and available supply.
2. Analyze each job and prepare job descriptions/specifications.
3. Recruit internally or externally.
4. Screen applications and assess candidates consistently.
5. Select, offer, onboard, and review results.

Recruitment builds a pool; selection chooses the best fit. Valid selection can combine structured interviews, work samples, tests, and reference checks. Decisions should be job-related, fair, documented, and free of discrimination.

### Eight-stage recruitment flow

`Workforce planning → Job analysis → Sourcing → Screening → Tests/interviews → Final selection → Offer/acceptance → Onboarding/induction`

- **Job description:** duties, responsibilities, reporting, and conditions.
- **Person/job specification:** qualifications, skills, experience, and attributes required.
- **Internal sourcing:** cheaper/faster and motivates staff, but limits the pool and may create another vacancy.
- **External sourcing:** brings fresh skills and diversity, but costs more and has greater fit uncertainty.
- Modern trends: social/digital recruitment, applicant-tracking/e-recruitment systems, employer branding, data-driven selection, global/remote hiring, and candidate-experience design.
- Lecture scenario: Virtusa's large software-engineer intake uses workforce planning, Java/Spring/AWS role analysis, LinkedIn/university/referral sourcing, CV screening, coding test, technical + HR interviews, and onboarding.

## Performance management

Performance management is a continuous cycle of goal setting, coaching, review, development, and recognition—not merely an annual appraisal.

- Use specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound (**SMART**) goals.
- Combine results with behaviors and competencies.
- Common rating errors include halo/horns effects, recency, leniency, central tendency, and personal bias.
- Constructive feedback should be timely, evidence-based, specific, and improvement-oriented.

The complete lecture cycle is **planning → acting → monitoring → reviewing → development**, then repeats. Key components are objectives/standards, KPIs, ongoing observation, communication and coaching, appraisal, gap identification, learning plans, and links to recognition/reward.

## Rewards

- **Extrinsic:** salary, incentives, benefits, promotion, and formal recognition.
- **Intrinsic:** achievement, autonomy, mastery, purpose, and meaningful responsibility.
- Reward systems require internal equity, external competitiveness, affordability, transparency, and alignment with desired behavior.

The **total reward** concept combines pay, incentives, benefits, recognition, career growth, learning, meaningful work, flexibility, and work–life balance. Google-style examples in the lecture include flexible work, innovation time, recognition, and development. Rewards can attract talent, reinforce performance, improve satisfaction/engagement, and reduce turnover; poorly designed individual incentives can also damage teamwork or encourage gaming.

## Motivation and personality

- **Maslow:** needs progress from basic/psychological security toward belonging, esteem, and self-actualization.
- **Herzberg:** hygiene factors reduce dissatisfaction; motivators create satisfaction and growth.
- **Expectancy theory:** effort increases when employees expect it to improve performance and lead to valued rewards.
- **Equity theory:** employees compare their input–outcome balance with others.
- Personality affects communication and work preferences, but labels should not replace observation or fair assessment.

## Leadership, power, and negotiation

Leadership styles include directive/autocratic, participative/democratic, delegative, transactional, transformational, and situational approaches. Effective leaders adapt to task, risk, competence, and team needs.

Negotiation should distinguish positions from interests, prepare alternatives (**BATNA**), seek objective criteria, generate options, and document agreements. Power may come from formal authority, reward, coercion, expertise, or relationships.

### Leadership versus management

| Leadership | Management |
|---|---|
| Inspires a shared direction | Plans, organizes, and controls resources |
| People and relationships | Tasks, systems, and consistency |
| Change, innovation, commitment | Stability, coordination, and efficiency |

- Lecture styles: **autocratic** (fast control in urgent situations), **democratic** (participation and commitment), and **transformational** (vision, inspiration, and change).
- An Agile leader/Scrum Master facilitates self-management, removes blockers, and enables collaboration; a traditional manager more directly assigns, directs, and monitors work.
- Negotiation modes: **collaborative/win–win**, **competitive/win–lose**, and **compromise**.
- Lecture power sources: **legitimate/position**, **expert**, and **referent/personal** power. Expert and referent power often remain influential without formal authority.

## Project teams

Teams commonly move through forming, storming, norming, performing, and adjourning. High-performing IT teams need clear roles, psychological safety, complementary skills, communication norms, accountability, conflict resolution, and shared goals.

Lecture team challenges include communication breakdown, role ambiguity, interpersonal/task conflict, lack of trust, weak leadership, and low commitment. A Scrum-team example combines developers, QA, UI/UX, and project leadership, working through sprints and daily stand-ups toward a time-bound deliverable.

## Personality and the Big Five

| Trait | Workplace tendency when strong |
|---|---|
| Openness | Curiosity, creativity, comfort with new ideas |
| Conscientiousness | Reliability, organization, responsibility |
| Extraversion | Sociability, energy, assertiveness |
| Agreeableness | Cooperation, trust, friendliness |
| Emotional stability | Calmness and resilience under pressure |

Personality insight can improve selection, team composition, leadership development, tailored feedback, conflict handling, and job fit. It should never become stereotyping or a substitute for skill evidence and observed behavior.

## Integrated Sri Lankan IT case

The IFS Sri Lanka case highlights high turnover, global competition for local talent, burnout/low motivation, and adaptation to new technologies. Responses include globally competitive/flexible rewards, career paths and retention incentives, stronger employer branding and university links, workload/well-being support, recognition, continuous cloud/AI training and certification, innovation time, regular feedback, and an integrated HR strategy aligned with technology goals.
